In an AI-assisted computational project on NYC Citi Bike, I investigated how demand forecasts translate into rebalancing decisions under imperfect operational evidence. The pipeline processed 45.6 million retained rides and compared four next-hour count predictors using chronological validation. XGBoost reduced pooled departure MAE from 4.476 to 3.418, but an evidence-stratified audit revealed a more instructive limitation: the last-hour baseline performed better on 10,819 test station-hours lacking fresh start-state support. A separately frozen January 2026 evaluation then tested unchanged models on 22,320 new station-hours, achieving departure MAE 2.705 against 3.235 for last-hour. The project also connected integer and stochastic optimization with paired-ride simulation and delayed truck routing. In the road-constrained scenario, optimized rebalancing did not outperform no intervention. These results exposed the distinction between predictive accuracy, a mathematical proxy objective and realized service under modeling assumptions. The resulting code, source manifests, negative results and 60 tests form a reproducible basis for further study of uncertainty-aware urban operations. A natural next step is prospective inventory validation and policy evaluation on a genuinely untouched period, rather than treating unobserved historical states as known.
